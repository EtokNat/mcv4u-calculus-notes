Increasing/Decreasing Functions and the First Derivative Test

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §4.1 — where f′ is positive the graph rises, where f′ is negative it falls; the first derivative test classifies critical points.

🎯 Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Use the sign of f′(x) to find the intervals where f increases or decreases.
  • Find critical numbers (where f′(c) = 0 or f′(c) is undefined) and build a sign chart.
  • Apply the first derivative test to identify local maxima, local minima, or neither.

💡 Minds On

Imagine hiking a trail: when the slope under your feet is positive you are climbing, when it is negative you are descending, and when it is zero you are on flat ground — possibly at the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley. A function's derivative is that slope. In this lesson you will turn the sign of f′(x) into a complete map of where a graph rises and falls, and you will learn to spot its hills and valleys exactly.

📖 Learn — step by step

The sign of f′ controls rise and fall

The derivative f′(x) is the slope of the tangent line at each point. A positive slope means the curve is heading upward as x increases; a negative slope means it is heading downward. So:

  • If f′(x) > 0 on an interval, then f is increasing there (the graph rises left to right).
  • If f′(x) < 0 on an interval, then f is decreasing there (the graph falls left to right).

The places where f′ can change sign are the critical numbers: interior points c of the domain where f′(c) = 0 or where f′(c) is undefined. These numbers split the domain into intervals. Because f′ cannot change sign without passing through zero (or a break), testing one x-value in each interval tells you the sign on the whole interval. A sign chart organizes this work.

Example 1 — Find the intervals on which f(x) = x³ − 3x² is increasing or decreasing.

Solution. Differentiate and find the critical numbers.
f′(x) = 3x² − 6x = 3x(x − 2)
f′(x) = 0 when x = 0 or x = 2. These split the domain into (−∞, 0), (0, 2), (2, ∞).

Test one value in each interval:
• x = −1: f′(−1) = 3(−1)(−3) = 9 > 0 → increasing on (−∞, 0)
• x = 1: f′(1) = 3(1)(−1) = −3 < 0 → decreasing on (0, 2)
• x = 3: f′(3) = 3(3)(1) = 9 > 0 → increasing on (2, ∞)

The first derivative test

A critical number is only a candidate for a local extremum. The first derivative test looks at how the sign of f′ changes at the critical number c:

  • f′ changes + → − at c → f has a local maximum at c.
  • f′ changes − → + at c → f has a local minimum at c.
  • f′ does not change sign at c → neither (the graph keeps rising or keeps falling through c).

Example 2 — Find the intervals of increase/decrease of f(x) = x/(x² + 1) and classify its local extrema.

Solution. Use the quotient rule.
f′(x) = [(1)(x² + 1) − x(2x)]/(x² + 1)² = (1 − x²)/(x² + 1)²
The denominator is always positive, so the sign of f′ is the sign of 1 − x². Critical numbers: x = −1, x = 1.

• On (−∞, −1): 1 − x² < 0 → decreasing
• On (−1, 1): 1 − x² > 0 → increasing
• On (1, ∞): 1 − x² < 0 → decreasing

First derivative test: at x = −1 the sign changes − → + → local minimum, f(−1) = −1/2. At x = 1 the sign changes + → − → local maximum, f(1) = 1/2.

Critical does not always mean extremum

Two warnings worth memorizing. First, a horizontal tangent is not enough: for y = x³, f′(0) = 0, yet the sign of f′ = 3x² is positive on both sides — the graph climbs straight through (0, 0). Second, critical numbers also occur where the derivative is undefined: y = x^(2/3) has a sharp cusp at x = 0 (f′ undefined there), and (0, 0) is a local minimum.

Example 3 — Find and classify the critical points of f(x) = x⁴ − 8x³ + 18x².

Solution.
f′(x) = 4x³ − 24x² + 36x = 4x(x² − 6x + 9) = 4x(x − 3)²
Critical numbers: x = 0 and x = 3. Note (x − 3)² ≥ 0 for all x, so the sign of f′ is the sign of 4x:

• On (−∞, 0): f′ < 0 → decreasing
• On (0, 3) and (3, ∞): f′ > 0 → increasing

At x = 0 the sign changes − → + → local minimum, f(0) = 0.
At x = 3 the sign does not change (+ → +) → neither a max nor a min, even though f′(3) = 0. (The graph flattens momentarily at (3, 27) and keeps rising.)

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Modelled on Exercise 4.1.

Question 1 — Find the intervals of increase/decrease of f(x) = x² − 4x + 3.

f′(x) = 2x − 4 = 0 at x = 2. Sign chart: f′ < 0 on (−∞, 2), f′ > 0 on (2, ∞). So f is decreasing on (−∞, 2) and increasing on (2, ∞), with a local minimum at (2, −1).

Question 2 — Find the intervals of increase/decrease of f(x) = x³ − 12x + 2, and classify its local extrema.

f′(x) = 3x² − 12 = 3(x − 2)(x + 2); critical numbers x = −2, 2. Sign: + on (−∞, −2), − on (−2, 2), + on (2, ∞). Increasing on (−∞, −2) and (2, ∞); decreasing on (−2, 2). Local maximum at (−2, 18); local minimum at (2, −14).

Question 3 — True or false: "If f′(c) = 0 then f has a local extremum at x = c." Justify.

False. Counterexample: f(x) = x³ has f′(0) = 0, but f′ = 3x² ≥ 0 on both sides of x = 0 — no sign change, so (0, 0) is neither a max nor a min. A critical number is only a candidate.

Question 4 — Find the intervals of increase/decrease of f(x) = (x − 1)/(x + 2).

f′(x) = [(1)(x + 2) − (x − 1)(1)]/(x + 2)² = 3/(x + 2)², which is positive wherever f is defined. So f is increasing on (−∞, −2) and on (−2, ∞) and never decreasing. (Note x = −2 is not a critical number — it isn't in the domain at all.)

📝 Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • f′(x) > 0 → f increasing; f′(x) < 0 → f decreasing. The sign chart of f′ maps every rise and fall.
  • Critical numbers: interior domain points where f′(c) = 0 or f′(c) is undefined.
  • First derivative test: + → − gives a local max; − → + gives a local min; no sign change gives neither.

Need to know

  • Always include points where f′ is undefined and points where f itself is undefined as dividers in your sign chart.

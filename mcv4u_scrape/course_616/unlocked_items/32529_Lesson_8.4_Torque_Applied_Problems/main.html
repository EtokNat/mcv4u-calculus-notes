Torque — Applied Problems

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §7.7 (applied) — wrenches, doors, and seesaws: turning force into turning effect.

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Compute the magnitude of torque for realistic tools and mechanisms.
  • Explain why where you push (and at what angle) changes the turning effect.
  • Solve rotational balance problems (seesaws, levers) by equating opposing torques.

Minds On

A rusted bolt won't budge with a short wrench — but a longer one frees it. Door handles sit as far from the hinges as possible. A light child balances a heavy sibling on a seesaw by sliding farther out. None of these change the force — they change the torque: τ = r × f, with magnitude |τ| = |r||f|sinθ, measured in newton-metres (N·m). Today is all about using that formula on real hardware.

Learn — step by step

The wrench: torque at an angle

Torque magnitude is |τ| = |r||f|sinθ, where r is the lever arm (pivot to point of application), f is the force, and θ is the angle between them. Maximum torque comes from pulling at 90°; pulling along the wrench (θ = 0°) gives none at all.

Example 1 — A mechanic pulls a wrench with 20 N of force at 60° to the wrench handle. The bolt (pivot) is 0.40 m from where the hand grips. What is the torque on the bolt?

Solution. |τ| = |r||f|sinθ = 0.40 × 20 × sin60° ≈ 8(0.8660) ≈ 6.93 N·m, directed into the page (tightening, by the right-hand rule).

The door: where you push matters

The same push produces very different torques depending on where along the door it is applied — because r, the distance from the hinges, changes. This is the whole reason door handles are placed far from the hinges.

Example 2 — You push a door with 40 N perpendicular to its surface. Compare the torque when you push (a) at the handle, 0.90 m from the hinges, and (b) halfway, 0.30 m from the hinges.

Solution. (a) |τ| = 0.90 × 40 × sin90° = 36 N·m.
(b) |τ| = 0.30 × 40 × sin90° = 12 N·m.
Pushing at the handle delivers three times the turning effect for the same effort.

Rotational balance: the seesaw

A lever balances when the clockwise and counterclockwise torques are equal — rotational equilibrium. Weights act downward at each rider's distance from the pivot; a lighter rider balances a heavier one by sitting farther out.

Example 3 — A 30 kg child sits 2.0 m from the pivot of a seesaw. Where must a 20 kg child sit on the other side to balance it?

Solution. Balance: (30)(9.8)(2.0) = (20)(9.8)(d).
The 9.8 cancels: 60 = 20d → d = 3.0 m from the pivot.
In general: the lighter rider sits farther out in inverse proportion to the weights.

Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Applied torque problems.

Question 1 — A 25 N force is applied to a 60 cm wrench at 30° to the handle. What is the torque magnitude?

|τ| = 0.60 × 25 × sin30° = 15(0.5) = 7.5 N·m.

Question 2 — A car's lug nut needs 90 N·m of torque. You can push with 300 N at 90° to the wrench. How long must the wrench be?

90 = r × 300 × sin90° → r = 90/300 = 0.30 m.

Question 3 — Why does a longer wrench help loosen a tight bolt, even though your hand pushes with the same force?

Torque is |τ| = |r||f|sinθ — it scales directly with the lever arm r. A longer wrench multiplies the same force into more torque at the bolt.

Question 4 — A 45 kg adult and a 25 kg child share a seesaw. The adult sits 1.2 m from the pivot. Where should the child sit to balance?

45(1.2) = 25d → d = 54/25 = 2.16 m from the pivot on the other side.

Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • |τ| = |r||f|sinθ — torque grows with lever arm, force, and the sine of the angle between them.
  • Pull at 90° and as far from the pivot as possible for maximum turning effect.
  • Rotational equilibrium: clockwise torques = counterclockwise torques.

Need to know

  • Torque units: N·m (same units as the joule, but a different physical quantity — don't call torque "energy").
  • The right-hand rule gives torque's direction: into or out of the page for planar problems.

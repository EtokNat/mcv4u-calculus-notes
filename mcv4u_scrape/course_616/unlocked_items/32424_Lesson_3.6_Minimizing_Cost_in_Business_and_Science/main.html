🎯 Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Build a cost function from per-unit material prices.
  • Use a volume constraint to reduce to one variable.
  • Find the minimum cost on a restricted domain and verify feasibility.

💡 Minds On

Minimizing cost works exactly like maximizing revenue — but the domain restrictions are often physical: a tank must fit inside its warehouse, a price must stay in 10-cent steps. In this lesson the constraint is a fixed volume (1000 m³) and the domain comes from the warehouse dimensions.

📖 Learn — step by step

Example 2 — A cylindrical chemical storage tank with a capacity of 1000 m³ is to be built in a warehouse 12 m by 15 m, with a height of 11 m. The base costs $100/m², the top $50/m², and the wall $80/m².
a. Determine whether a tank of this capacity can fit in the warehouse. If so, state the restrictions on the radius.
b. If it fits, determine the proportions that minimize the cost of the steel. (Two decimal places.)

Solution.
a. The radius cannot exceed 6 m (half the 12 m width), and the height cannot exceed 11 m. The largest possible tank has volume V = π(6)²(11) ≈ 1244 m³ ≥ 1000 m³, so the tank fits.
Limits on the radius: 0 < r ≤ 6. Also, with h = 11, πr²(11) ≥ 1000 gives r ≥ 5.38. So the radius must satisfy 5.38 ≤ r ≤ 6.
b. If the height is h metres and the radius is r metres:
• cost of the base = $100(πr²),
• cost of the top = $50(πr²),
• cost of the wall = $80(2πrh).
Total cost: C = 150πr² + 160πrh. Since V = πr²h = 1000, h = 1000/(πr²). Substituting:
C(r) = 150πr² + 160πr(1000/(πr²)) = 150πr² + 160 000/r, on 5.38 ≤ r ≤ 6.
Set C′(r) = 0: 300πr − 160 000/r² = 0 → 300πr = 160 000/r² → r³ = 1600/(3π) → r ≈ 5.54.
This is inside the domain, so apply the extreme-value algorithm:
C(5.38) = 150π(5.38)² + 160 000/5.38 ≈ 43 380;
C(5.54) = 150π(5.54)² + 160 000/5.54 ≈ 43 344;
C(6) = 150π(6)² + 160 000/6 ≈ 43 631.
The minimum cost is about $43 344, at radius 5.54 m with height h = 1000/(π(5.54)²) ≈ 10.37 m.

✅ Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Modelled on Exercise 3.4.

Question 1 — Maple-syrup cost is C(x) = 75√(x − 10) dollars, x ≥ 400 L. (a) Average cost of 625 L. (b) Marginal cost at 1225 L. (c) Production level for marginal cost $0.50/L.

(a) C(625)/625 = 75√615/625 ≈ $2.98/L. (b) C′(x) = 75/(2√(x − 10)); C′(1225) ≈ $1.08/L — the expected cost of one more litre. (c) 75/(2√(x − 10)) = 0.50 → √(x − 10) = 75 → x = 5635 L.

Question 2 — A laptop maker sells x thousand units at (2000 − 5x) dollars each; cost is C(x) = 15 000 000 + 1 800 000x + 75x² dollars. What sales level maximizes profit?

R(x) = x(2000 − 5x) thousand dollars = 2000x − 5x² (in thousands). P(x) = R − C (in thousands) = −5x² + 200x − 15 000 − 1800x − 0.075x²... working in dollars: R = 1000x(2000 − 5x) = 2 000 000x − 5000x². P′ = 2 000 000 − 10 000x − 1 800 000 − 150x = 200 000 − 10 150x = 0 → x ≈ 19.7 thousand units.

Question 3 — Antacid level is L(t) = 6t/(t² + 2t + 1), t minutes after the tablet. At what t is L′(t) = 0, and what is L(t) there?

L′(t) = [6(t² + 2t + 1) − 6t(2t + 2)]/(t² + 2t + 1)² = (6 − 6t²)/(t + 1)⁴ = 0 → t = 1 min; L(1) = 6/4 = 1.5.

Question 4 — What does it mean for marginal cost to equal $0.50/L at some production level?

Producing one more litre at that level is expected to cost about $0.50 — marginal cost is the instantaneous rate of change of total cost.

📝 Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • Build the cost function from per-unit prices; use the fixed-volume (or fixed-area) constraint to get one variable.
  • Check feasibility first — the domain comes from the real-world container, not just the math.

Need to know

  • Apply the extreme-value algorithm on the restricted domain; compare critical points and endpoints.
  • Round only at the end; keep full precision through the algebra.

Radical Expressions: Rationalizing Denominators

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §1.1 — the algebra skill every limit in this unit depends on.

🎯 Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Rewrite a fraction so that a one-term radical denominator becomes a whole number, by multiplying top and bottom by that radical.
  • Rewrite a fraction with a two-term radical denominator by multiplying by the conjugate, and simplify fully.
  • Rationalize a numerator — the move you will reuse when you meet difference quotients with radicals.

💡 Minds On

A rational number is one you can write as a fraction (quotient) of integers. A denominator like √5 is not rational, and mathematicians long ago agreed it should not stay there: dividing by an integer is preferable to dividing by a radical. So before calculus begins, we master the clean-up move called rationalizing the denominator — changing a radical denominator into an integer. There are two situations to handle, depending on whether the denominator has one term or two, and the same idea later lets you rationalize a numerator. In both cases the trick is the same: multiply the numerator and denominator by the same expression — which is really just multiplying by 1, so the value never changes.

📖 Learn — step by step

Situation 1: a one-term denominator

When the denominator is a single radical term, multiply the numerator and denominator by that same radical. The denominator becomes a whole number:

Example 1 — Simplify 3/(4√5) by rationalizing the denominator.

Solution.
3/(4√5) = 3/(4√5) × √5/√5   (multiply top and bottom by √5)
= 3√5/(4 × 5)   (simplify)
= 3√5/20

Why conjugates matter

When the denominator has two terms, one radical is not enough — you need the conjugate. An expression such as √a + √b has the conjugate √a − √b (same terms, sign flipped).

Conjugates work because of the difference of squares: expanding (a − b)(a + b) gives a² + ab − ab − b², and the middle terms cancel, leaving a² − b². The same thing happens with radicals — for instance (√m + √n)(√m − √n) = m − n. Notice that the result is rational! Squaring the radicals clears them out, which is exactly what we need.

Example 2 — Simplify 2/(√6 + √3) by rationalizing the denominator.

Solution.
2/(√6 + √3) = 2/(√6 + √3) × (√6 − √3)/(√6 − √3)   (multiply top and bottom by the conjugate √6 − √3)
= 2(√6 − √3)/(6 − 3)   (simplify — the denominator is a difference of squares)
= 2(√6 − √3)/3

Example 3 — Simplify the radical expression 5/(2√6 + 3) by rationalizing the denominator.

Solution.
5/(2√6 + 3) = 5/(2√6 + 3) × (2√6 − 3)/(2√6 − 3)   (the conjugate of 2√6 + 3 is 2√6 − 3)
= 5(2√6 − 3)/(4√36 − 9)   (simplify)
= 5(2√6 − 3)/(24 − 9)
= 5(2√6 − 3)/15   (divide by the common factor of 5)
= (2√6 − 3)/3

Need to know: when you simplify a radical expression such as √3/(5√2), multiply the numerator and denominator by the radical only:
√3/(5√2) × √2/√2 = √6/(5(2)) = √6/10

Rationalizing the numerator

In certain situations it is useful to rationalize the numerator instead of the denominator. Practice with rationalizing the denominator prepares you for it — the numerator is rationalized in the same way as the denominator was in the previous examples.

Example 4 — Rationalize the numerator of the expression (√7 − √3)/2.

Solution.
(√7 − √3)/2 = (√7 − √3)/2 × (√7 + √3)/(√7 + √3)   (multiply top and bottom by √7 + √3)
= (7 − 3)/(2(√7 + √3))   (simplify)
= 4/(2(√7 + √3))   (divide by the common factor of 2)
= 2/(√7 + √3)

✅ Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal the answers. They are modelled on Exercise 1.1.

Question 1 — Write the conjugate of (a) 5 + √2, (b) √11 − 2√5.

(a) 5 − √2; (b) √11 + 2√5 — same terms, flipped sign.

Question 2 — Rationalize 1/(√a − √b).

Multiply by (√a + √b)/(√a + √b): (√a + √b)/(a − b).

Question 3 — Rationalize the denominator of 6/(3√2).

6/(3√2) × √2/√2 = 6√2/(3 × 2) = 6√2/6 = √2.

Question 4 — Rationalize 4/(√5 − 1).

Conjugate is √5 + 1: 4(√5 + 1)/(5 − 1) = 4(√5 + 1)/4 = √5 + 1.

Question 5 — Rationalize the numerator of (√5 + 2)/3.

Multiply by (√5 − 2)/(√5 − 2): (5 − 4)/(3(√5 − 2)) = 1/(3(√5 − 2)).

📝 Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • One-term radical denominator: multiply the top and bottom by that same radical. The denominator squares into a whole number — e.g. √a/√b × √b/√b = √(ab)/b.
  • Two-term denominator: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate, then simplify. The difference of squares clears the radicals: 1/(√a − √b) × (√a + √b)/(√a + √b) = (√a + √b)/(a − b).

Need to know

  • With a coefficient in front, e.g. √3/(5√2), multiply by the radical only: √6/10.
  • √a + √b is the conjugate of √a − √b, and vice versa.
  • The numerator can be rationalized exactly the same way — watch for this move in Lesson 1.2's difference quotients.

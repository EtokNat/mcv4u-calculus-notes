Velocity and Navigation

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §7.2 (applied) — ground velocity, river crossings, and the headings pilots and captains actually steer.

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Distinguish airspeed/velocity through the water from ground velocity and combine them with the wind/current vector.
  • Solve river-crossing problems using similar velocity and distance triangles.
  • Determine the heading needed to travel along a desired course when wind or current is present.

Minds On

A plane points due north, but a strong wind blows — and the flight path curves off course. A ferry aims straight across a river, but the current sweeps it downstream. In both cases the vehicle moves with the vector sum of its own motion and the medium's motion. Pilots and captains don't just point and go: they solve a vector triangle before they depart. Today you'll learn to do the same.

Learn — step by step

Ground velocity: the sum of two motions

A vehicle's velocity relative to the medium (airspeed for a plane, velocity through the water for a boat) is what its engine produces. The ground velocity — the motion an observer on the ground actually sees — is the vector sum of that and the wind or current vector:
ground velocity = vehicle velocity + wind/current vector.

Terminology matters: a "wind from the northeast" blows toward the southwest, so its vector points southwest. Bearings measure clockwise from north: a course of 090° is due east. A heading is where the nose points; the course (track) is where you actually go.

Example 1 — A plane flies due north at an airspeed of 400 km/h. The wind blows from the northeast at 100 km/h. Determine the plane's ground velocity (magnitude and direction).

Solution. The wind vector points toward the southwest: w = 100(cos225°, sin225°) = (−70.71, −70.71).
Ground velocity g = v + w = (0 − 70.71, 400 − 70.71) = (−70.71, 329.29).
|g| = √(70.71² + 329.29²) ≈ √(5000 + 108432) ≈ 336.8 km/h.
Direction: arctan(70.71/329.29) ≈ 12.1° west of north — the plane drifts left of its heading.

Crossing a river: similar triangles

When a boat aims straight across a river, the current carries it downstream; its path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle. Because distance = speed × time for each leg, the distance triangle is similar to the velocity triangle — their ratios match, letting you find landing point, crossing time, and actual speed with simple proportions.

Example 2 — A motorboat can travel at 20 km/h in still water. It heads straight across a 2 km wide river whose current flows at 6 km/h. How far downstream does it land, and how long does the crossing take?

Solution. The crossing speed is the boat's speed straight across: time = distance/speed = 2/20 = 0.1 h = 6 min.
During those 6 min the current carries it 6(0.1) = 0.6 km downstream.
Actual path length: √(2² + 0.6²) = √4.36 ≈ 2.09 km; actual speed √(20² + 6²) ≈ 20.88 km/h.

Correcting your heading

To land straight across (or stay on a compass course), the captain must aim upstream so the current's drift cancels. In the velocity triangle, the desired course is one leg, the current is the other leg, and the boat's heading is the hypotenuse. Solve for the correction angle — then check it is physically possible (the current can't exceed the boat's speed).

Example 3 — The same boat (20 km/h) now wants to land directly across the 2 km river. On what heading should it steer, and how long will the crossing take?

Solution. The boat's heading is the hypotenuse of the velocity triangle; the current (6 km/h) is one leg: sinθ = 6/20 → θ = 17.5° upstream from straight across.
Resultant speed straight across: √(20² − 6²) = √364 ≈ 19.08 km/h.
Crossing time: 2/19.08 ≈ 0.105 h ≈ 6.3 min — slightly longer than the uncorrected crossing.

Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Applied navigation problems.

Question 1 — A train travels east at 84 km/h. A passenger walks toward the back of the train at 2 km/h. What is the passenger's ground velocity?

Ground velocity = 84 − 2 = 82 km/h east.

Question 2 — Judy can swim at 3 km/h in still water. She swims straight across a river 0.4 km wide flowing at 5 km/h. How far downstream will she be swept when she reaches the far side?

Crossing time = 0.4/3 ≈ 0.1333 h. Downstream drift = 5(0.1333) ≈ 0.667 km.

Question 3 — Barbara can swim at only 2 km/h and tries to swim straight upstream in a river flowing at 3 km/h. Can she make progress? Explain with vectors.

No. Her velocity through the water (2 km/h upstream) plus the current (3 km/h downstream) gives a ground velocity of 1 km/h downstream — she moves backward.

Question 4 — A plane flies due west at an airspeed of 300 km/h with a wind of 50 km/h from the south. Find its ground velocity.

Wind from the south blows north: w = (0, 50); v = (−300, 0). Ground velocity = (−300, 50): magnitude √(90000+2500) ≈ 304.1 km/h, direction arctan(50/300) ≈ 9.5° north of west.

Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • Ground velocity = vehicle velocity + wind/current vector — always check whether "from" means the vector points the other way.
  • River problems use similar triangles: the distance triangle matches the velocity triangle.
  • To hold a course, aim upstream; the correction angle comes from the velocity triangle (sinθ = current/speed).

Need to know

  • If the current exceeds your speed, landing upstream of the drift is impossible — recheck the triangle.
  • Headings are measured where the nose points; courses are where you actually travel.

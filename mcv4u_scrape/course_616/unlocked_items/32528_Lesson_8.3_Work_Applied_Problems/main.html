Work — Applied Problems

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §7.7 (applied) — ramps, mowers, and teams of pushers: computing work in real situations.

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Compute the work done by a force at an angle to the displacement in applied scenarios.
  • Combine the work of multiple forces (pushers, gravity, friction) into net work.
  • Interpret negative work and choose pull/push strategies that maximize work.

Minds On

A mover drags a crate up a ramp with a rope angled upward — only part of the pull helps; the rest lifts the rope uselessly. A lawnmower pushed at 45° wastes half the effort into the ground. Two friends push a stalled car at slightly different angles — how much does each actually contribute? The dot product W = F·s answers all of these. (Work is the dot product of the force and displacement vectors: W = F·s = |F||s|cosθ, measured in joules.)

Learn — step by step

Work on a ramp

Work is done only by the component of the force along the displacement: W = |F||s|cosθ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. On a ramp, the displacement follows the slope while the rope usually angles upward — so θ is the rope's angle above the ramp direction.

Example 1 — A mover pulls a crate up a ramp with a 140 N force on a rope angled 20° above the ramp surface. The crate moves 8 m up the ramp. How much work does the pull do?

Solution. W = |F||s|cosθ = 140 × 8 × cos20° ≈ 1120(0.9397) ≈ 1052 J.

Pushing vs pulling: the mower problem

The same effort can do very different work depending on direction. Pushing a mower handle at 45° drives a large component into the ground — work that presses the mower down but moves it nowhere. Only the horizontal component counts.

Example 2 — A gardener pushes a mower with 100 N of force at 45° below the horizontal, mowing a 500 m path. How much work is done? How much effort is "wasted" pressing into the ground?

Solution. Only the horizontal component moves the mower: F∥ = 100cos45° ≈ 70.71 N.
W = 70.71 × 500 ≈ 35,355 J.
The downward component, 100sin45° ≈ 70.71 N, is perpendicular to the displacement — it does no work (cos90° = 0) and only presses the mower harder into the grass.

Multiple forces, net work

Real jobs involve teams — and friction. The net work is the work of the resultant force: add the force vectors first, then dot with the displacement. A force opposing the motion (like friction) has θ = 180°, so its work is negative.

Example 3 — Two friends push a stalled car 20 m along a road. One pushes with 300 N at 10° to the road; the other pushes with 250 N at 15° to the road on the other side. Rolling resistance opposes the motion with 150 N. What is the net work done on the car?

Solution. Effective forward components: 300cos10° ≈ 295.42 N and 250cos15° ≈ 241.48 N.
(The sideways components cancel — that is why they push at symmetric-ish angles.)
Net forward force = 295.42 + 241.48 − 150 = 386.90 N.
Net work = 386.90 × 20 ≈ 7,738 J.

Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Applied work problems.

Question 1 — A toboggan is pulled 50 m with a 180 N force, doing 3654 J of work. At what angle above the snow was the rope pulled?

3654 = 180 × 50 × cosθ → cosθ = 3654/9000 ≈ 0.406 → θ ≈ 66°.

Question 2 — A mover pushes a stove 3 m across a floor against a friction force of 150 N. How much work does friction do? What sign is it, and why?

W = 150 × 3 × cos180° = −450 J. The negative sign shows friction removes energy from the motion — the mover must supply +450 J to compensate.

Question 3 — A wagon is pulled 250 m with a 140 N force at 20° above the horizontal. How much work is done?

W = 140 × 250 × cos20° ≈ 35000(0.9397) ≈ 32,890 J.

Question 4 — A delivery driver can pull a loaded cart with a fixed 200 N effort. Should the handle be kept as low as possible? Explain using the work formula.

Yes. W = |F||s|cosθ is maximized when cosθ is largest — θ = 0°, handle parallel to the motion. Every degree of lift shrinks the useful component and does nothing but fight gravity.

Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • W = F·s = |F||s|cosθ — only the force component along the displacement does work.
  • With several forces, find the resultant (net force) first, then compute one dot product — or add each force's work.
  • Opposing forces (friction) do negative work: θ = 180°.

Need to know

  • Work is measured in joules (1 J = 1 N·m).
  • θ is the angle between the force and the displacement, not the ground — on a ramp, use the rope's angle above the slope.

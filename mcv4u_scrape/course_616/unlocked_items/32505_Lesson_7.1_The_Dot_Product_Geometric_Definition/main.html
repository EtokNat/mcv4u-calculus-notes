The Dot Product — Geometric Definition

🎯 Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Compute the dot product a·b = |a||b|cosθ from two magnitudes and the angle between them (vectors placed tail to tail).
  • Read the sign of the dot product to tell whether the angle is acute, right, or obtuse.
  • Use the fact that nonzero vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is zero.
  • Apply the four dot-product properties: commutative, distributive, a·a = |a|², and (ka)·b = k(a·b).

💡 Minds On

Imagine a tug-of-war team pulling a rope that is not quite pointing where they want to go. Only the part of the pull along the rope's direction actually does anything. The dot product is the mathematical version of that idea: it combines two vectors and the angle between them into a single number — a scalar — that measures how much the two vectors "agree" with each other.

📖 Learn — step by step

The geometric definition

A geometric vector is a vector with no coordinate system attached — just a length and a direction. For any two vectors a and b, the dot product is defined by

a · b = |a| |b| cosθ,   0° ≤ θ ≤ 180°

where θ is the angle between the vectors when they are placed tail to tail — their starting points together. The angle θ is always taken between 0° and 180° (measuring in degrees or radians both work). The figure below shows the convention:

A scalar, not a vector

Every quantity on the right-hand side — |a|, |b|, and cosθ — is just a number, so their product is just a number. The dot product is therefore a scalar, which is why it is also called the scalar product. Two very different-looking calculations can give the same scalar.

The sign of the dot product

Because |a| and |b| are always positive (for nonzero vectors), the sign of a·b is entirely decided by cosθ:

  • 0° < θ < 90° — cosθ > 0, so a·b > 0 (the vectors lean the same way).
  • θ = 90° — cosθ = 0, so a·b = 0 (the vectors are perpendicular).
  • 90° < θ ≤ 180° — cosθ < 0, so a·b < 0 (the vectors lean opposite ways).

Key term — Perpendicular test: two nonzero vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is zero. (The "nonzero" matters: the zero vector has a zero dot product with everything, but we do not call it perpendicular to anything.)

Properties of the dot product

Four rules make the dot product behave like ordinary multiplication in most ways — but note what is missing: there is no way to "divide" by a vector, and a·(b·c) is meaningless (b·c is a scalar, and you cannot dot a vector with a scalar).

  • Commutative: a·b = b·a. (The definition multiplies |a||b|cosθ — scalars multiply in any order.)
  • Distributive: a·(b + c) = a·b + a·c.
  • Magnitudes property: a·a = |a|². (Because θ = 0° and cos0° = 1.) This lets you recover a length from a dot product: |a| = √(a·a).
  • Scalar factor: (ka)·b = a·(kb) = k(a·b) — a scalar factor can move freely in and out.

Example 1 — Two vectors a and b are placed tail to tail with magnitudes 3 and 5, respectively, and an angle of 120° between them. Calculate a·b.

a·b = |a||b|cosθ = (3)(5)cos120°. Since cos120° = −0.5, a·b = 15(−0.5) = −7.5. The negative sign makes sense: 120° is obtuse, so the vectors lean opposite ways.

Example 2 — If |a| = 2|b|, and the vectors a + 3b and 4a − b are perpendicular, determine the angle between the nonzero vectors a and b (nearest 0.1°).

Perpendicular vectors have a zero dot product, so (a + 3b)·(4a − b) = 0. Expanding with the distributive property: 4(a·a) − a·b + 12(b·a) − 3(b·b) = 0. Using commutativity (b·a = a·b) and the magnitudes property: 4|a|² + 11(a·b) − 3|b|² = 0.
Since |a| = 2|b|, we have |a|² = 4|b|², and a·b = |a||b|cosθ = 2|b|²cosθ. Substituting: 4(4|b|²) + 11(2|b|²cosθ) − 3|b|² = 0, i.e. 13|b|² + 22|b|²cosθ = 0. Dividing by |b|² (b is nonzero): cosθ = −13/22 ≈ −0.5909, so θ ≈ 126.2°.

Example 3 — If |x + y| = |x − y|, prove that the nonzero vectors x and y are perpendicular.

Square both sides: |x + y|² = |x − y|². The left side is (x + y)·(x + y) = |x|² + 2(x·y) + |y|²; the right side is (x − y)·(x − y) = |x|² − 2(x·y) + |y|². Equating: |x|² + 2(x·y) + |y|² = |x|² − 2(x·y) + |y|², which simplifies to 4(x·y) = 0, so x·y = 0. Since x and y are nonzero, a zero dot product means they are perpendicular.

✅ Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Modelled on Exercise 7.3.

Question 1 — |p| = 4, |q| = 8, and the angle between them (tail to tail) is 60°. Find p·q.

p·q = (4)(8)cos60° = 32(0.5) = 16.

Question 2 — If a·b = 0, are a and b necessarily perpendicular? Explain.

Not necessarily — you need both vectors to be nonzero. If a = 0, then a·b = 0 no matter what b is, but the zero vector is not called perpendicular to anything.

Question 3 — Two unit vectors point in exactly opposite directions. What is their dot product?

θ = 180°, so a·b = (1)(1)cos180° = −1.

Question 4 — Simplify (a + 5b)·(2a − 3b) using the dot-product properties.

Distribute: 2(a·a) − 3(a·b) + 10(b·a) − 15(b·b) = 2|a|² + 7(a·b) − 15|b|² (using commutativity to combine the middle terms).

📝 Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • a·b = |a||b|cosθ with θ measured tail to tail, 0° ≤ θ ≤ 180° — the result is a scalar.
  • Sign of a·b: positive (acute), zero (right), negative (obtuse).
  • Nonzero vectors are perpendicular ⟺ a·b = 0.

Need to know

  • Four properties: commutative, distributive, a·a = |a|², scalar-factor rule.
  • You cannot take the dot product of a vector with a scalar — (a·b)·c is meaningless.

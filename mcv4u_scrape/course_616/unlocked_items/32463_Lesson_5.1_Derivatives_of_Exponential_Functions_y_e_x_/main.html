Derivatives of Exponential Functions (y = ex)

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §5.1 — the number e, why ex is its own derivative, and the chain rule with exponentials.

🎯 Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Explain why the power rule does not apply to y = ex, and state the value of e.
  • Use d/dx(ex) = ex — the function is its own derivative — and explain what it means for tangent slopes.
  • Apply the chain rule to differentiate eg(x), alone or combined with the product and quotient rules.
  • Find equations of tangent lines to exponential curves.

💡 Minds On

You already know how to differentiate x3 (power rule: bring down the 3). Now look at ex: the variable is in the exponent, not the base. The power rule is built for a constant exponent — here the exponent itself changes, so the power rule is the wrong tool entirely. This lesson introduces a brand-new number, e, and a brand-new rule — and the rule turns out to be the simplest one in all of calculus: the derivative of ex is ex itself.

📖 Learn — step by step

Meet e: the natural number

The number e ≈ 2.718 is an irrational number (like π), named Euler's number after the mathematician Leonhard Euler. It is the "natural" base for exponential functions because of one remarkable property: y = ex is its own derivative. If you tabulate ex and its (numerical) derivative at x = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, the two columns match. Since a derivative is a tangent slope, this says: the slope of the tangent to y = ex at any point equals the value of the function at that point — at (1, e) the slope is e; at (2, e²) the slope is e²; and so on.

RULE — Derivative of ex
If f(x) = ex, then f′(x) = ex. In Leibniz notation: d/dx(ex) = ex.

The exponential y = ex has a mirror twin: its inverse is y = ln x (the natural logarithm), and their graphs are reflections of each other in the line y = x. Remember the swap: ex has domain all real numbers and range y > 0, while ln x has domain x > 0 and range all real numbers.

The chain rule with eg(x)

Most exponential functions in the wild are composites — the exponent is itself a function. The chain rule gives a clean result: copy the exponential, then multiply by the derivative of the exponent.

RULE — Derivative of eg(x)
If f(x) = eg(x), then f′(x) = eg(x) · g′(x).

Example 1 — Determine the derivative of f(x) = e3x.

Solution. Use the chain rule with g(x) = 3x, g′(x) = 3.
f′(x) = e3x · 3 = 3e3x.

Example 2 — Determine the derivative of (a) g(x) = ex²−x and (b) h(x) = x²ex.

Solution.
(a) Chain rule with g(x) = x² − x: g′(x) = ex²−x · (2x − 1).

(b) Product rule: h′(x) = (2x)(ex) + (x²)(ex) = ex(2x + x²). Always factor the ex out at the end — factored form is what you need for sign analysis and solving f′ = 0 later.

Tangents to exponential curves

The tangent recipe is unchanged: differentiate for the slope, evaluate the point, write the line. One handy simplification — rewrite a quotient like ex/x² as the product x−2ex before differentiating.

Example 3 — Determine the equation of the line tangent to y = ex/x² where x = 2.

Solution. Rewrite as y = x−2ex, then use the product rule:
y′ = (−2x−3)(ex) + (x−2)(ex) = [(x − 2)ex]/x³.

At x = 2: y = e²/4, and y′ = (0·e²)/8 = 0. The tangent is horizontal:
y = e²/4.

✅ Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Modelled on Exercise 5.1.

Question 1 — Differentiate y = e5x.

Chain rule: the exponent's derivative is 5, so y′ = 5e5x.

Question 2 — Differentiate f(x) = xe−x.

Product rule: f′(x) = (1)(e−x) + (x)(−e−x) = e−x(1 − x).

Question 3 — True or false: "The power rule gives d/dx(ex) = x·ex−1." Justify.

False. The power rule applies when the exponent is constant (xn with fixed n). In ex the variable is in the exponent, so the rule does not apply — the correct derivative is ex.

Question 4 — Find the equation of the tangent to y = e2x at x = 0.

Point: (0, 1). Slope: y′ = 2e2x, so y′(0) = 2. Tangent: y − 1 = 2x, i.e. y = 2x + 1.

📝 Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • e ≈ 2.718 is Euler's (natural) number; the power rule cannot differentiate ex because the exponent is variable.
  • d/dx(ex) = ex — the tangent slope at any point equals the function's value there.
  • d/dx(eg(x)) = eg(x)·g′(x) — copy the exponential, multiply by the exponent's derivative.
  • Combine with product/quotient rules freely; factor e(…) out of the final answer.

Need to know

  • y = ex and y = ln x are inverses — reflections in y = x (domains and ranges swap).

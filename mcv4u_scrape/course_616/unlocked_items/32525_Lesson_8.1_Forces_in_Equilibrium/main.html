Forces in Equilibrium

Nelson Calculus and Vectors 12 §7.1 (applied) — combining real forces into a resultant, and balancing them into equilibrium.

Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Find the resultant of several real-world forces (towing, pushing, pulling) using vector addition.
  • Determine the equilibrant — the single force that balances a system.
  • Solve static equilibrium problems (hanging masses, ramps) with force triangles and component balance.

Learn — step by step

Resultant and equilibrant in the real world

A force is a push or pull described by magnitude (in newtons, N) and direction — a vector. On Earth, a mass of m kilograms weighs about 9.8·m newtons downward. When several forces act at once, the single force with the same combined effect is the resultant (the vector sum). The single force that would exactly oppose the resultant and hold everything still is the equilibrant: same magnitude, opposite direction. A system is in equilibrium when its forces sum to zero — no motion results.

Example 1 — Two tugboats tow a barge. Tug 1 pulls with 30 kN at 20° above the forward direction; tug 2 pulls with 40 kN at 25° below the forward direction. Determine the resultant pull on the barge (magnitude and direction), and the equilibrant.

Solution. Resolve each tow force into forward (x) and sideways (y) components, taking forward as the x-axis.
T1: x = 30cos20° ≈ 28.19 kN, y = 30sin20° ≈ 10.26 kN
T2: x = 40cos(−25°) ≈ 36.25 kN, y = 40sin(−25°) ≈ −16.90 kN
Add components: Rx ≈ 28.19 + 36.25 = 64.44 kN; Ry ≈ 10.26 − 16.90 = −6.64 kN.
|R| = √(64.44² + 6.64²) ≈ √(4152.5 + 44.1) ≈ 64.78 kN.
Direction: θ = arctan(−6.64/64.44) ≈ −5.9° (5.9° below the forward direction).
The equilibrant is 64.78 kN in the opposite direction (5.9° above the backward direction) — the single force that would hold the barge still.

Equilibrium: the triangle of forces

Three noncollinear forces in equilibrium always lie in one plane and, arranged head-to-tail, close into a triangle: a + b + c = 0. A quick feasibility test is the triangle inequality — the two smaller magnitudes must add to at least the largest, or no triangle (and no equilibrium) is possible. Two standard solution strategies: the triangle of forces (sine/cosine law on the closed triangle) and component balance (horizontal components sum to zero; vertical components sum to zero).

Example 2 — A 20 kg chandelier (weight 196 N) hangs from two chains that make angles of 60° and 45° with the ceiling. Determine the tension in each chain.

Solution. Let the tensions be T1 (60° side) and T2 (45° side). In equilibrium the three forces — T1, T2, and the 196 N weight — form a closed triangle. The triangle's angles are 30°, 45°, and 105°.
By the sine law: T1/sin45° = T2/sin30° = 196/sin105°.
T1 = 196·sin45°/sin105° ≈ 196(0.7071)/0.9659 ≈ 143.5 N
T2 = 196·sin30°/sin105° ≈ 196(0.5)/0.9659 ≈ 101.5 N
Check by components: horizontal balance needs T1cos60° = T2cos45° → 143.5(0.5) ≈ 71.7 and 101.5(0.7071) ≈ 71.7 ✓; vertical: T1sin60° + T2sin45° ≈ 124.3 + 71.7 = 196 ✓.

Forces on a ramp

A block resting on an incline is a classic equilibrium setup: gravity pulls straight down, but the ramp only "feels" part of it. Resolving the weight into components parallel and perpendicular to the ramp tells you both the force trying to slide the block and the force pressing it into the surface.

Example 3 — A 10 kg crate (weight 98 N) sits on a smooth ramp inclined at 30°. What force, parallel to the ramp, would keep the crate from sliding? What force does the crate exert perpendicular to the ramp?

Solution. The component of the weight along the ramp is 98·sin30° = 98(0.5) = 49 N down the slope — so a 49 N force up the ramp holds it in equilibrium.
The component pressing into the ramp is 98·cos30° ≈ 98(0.8660) ≈ 84.9 N.

Check Your Understanding

Try these on paper first, then reveal. Applied statics problems.

Question 1 — A moored boat is pulled by a 5 kN rope due east and a 12 kN rope due north. What single rope (magnitude and direction) would hold the boat in exactly the same position against both pulls — i.e. what is the equilibrant?

Resultant: |R| = √(5² + 12²) = 13 kN at arctan(12/5) ≈ 67.4° north of east. The equilibrant is 13 kN in the opposite direction (67.4° south of west).

Question 2 — Three cables pull on a pylon with forces of 15 kN, 18 kN, and 38 kN. Can the pylon be in equilibrium under just these three forces? Explain.

No. Equilibrium needs a closed force triangle, but 15 + 18 = 33 < 38 violates the triangle inequality — no triangle with these side lengths exists.

Question 3 — A 60 kg person does chin-ups. Explain with a force diagram why it is easier with hands 30 cm apart than 90 cm apart, assuming each arm exerts the same force.

Each arm's force has a vertical component supporting the weight. With hands wide apart, each arm pulls at a large angle from vertical, so the vertical component (F·cosθ) is smaller — the arms must pull harder overall to supply the same upward total. Hands close together keep the pull nearly vertical, maximizing the useful component.

Question 4 — A traffic light weighing 120 N hangs from two identical cables that each make 75° with the horizontal. What is the tension in each cable?

By symmetry each cable carries half the vertical load: 2·T·sin75° = 120 → T = 60/sin75° ≈ 60/0.9659 ≈ 62.1 N.

Lesson Summary

Key ideas

  • The resultant is the vector sum of all forces — find it by adding components or with the cosine/sine laws on a force diagram.
  • The equilibrant is equal and opposite to the resultant; a system is in equilibrium when its forces sum to zero.
  • Three forces in equilibrium form a closed triangle (triangle inequality must hold); solve with the sine law or by balancing horizontal and vertical components.

Need to know

  • Always draw the force diagram first — position diagram (real layout) and vector diagram (head-to-tail).
  • On a ramp, weight splits into W·sinθ along the slope and W·cosθ into the slope.

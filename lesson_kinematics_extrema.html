Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you can:

  • Relate position \(s(t)\), velocity \(v(t) = s'(t)\), and acceleration \(a(t) = s''(t)\).
  • Decide when an object is speeding up or slowing down.
  • Apply the extreme-value algorithm on a closed interval.
  • Decide when the extreme-value algorithm does not apply.

Minds On

Derivatives give us rates of change — for motion, that's velocity (first derivative of position) and acceleration (second derivative).

Next, we'll ask a different question: on a closed interval, where does a function reach its largest and smallest values? At a peak or valley — or at an endpoint?

Part 1 — Motion on a Line

Position, velocity, acceleration

For an object moving on a straight line with position \(s(t)\):

\(v(t) = s'(t) = \dfrac{ds}{dt}\)    (velocity)

\(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = \dfrac{d^{2}s}{dt^{2}}\)    (acceleration)

Speed \(= |v(t)|\).

Sign conventions: \(v>0\) move right; \(v<0\) move left; \(v=0\) at rest.

Speeding up when \(v\) and \(a\) share a sign (\(va > 0\)); slowing down when signs differ (\(va < 0\)).

Example — \(s(t) = 6t^{2} - t^{3}\),   \(t \ge 0\)

Position in metres; time in seconds.

(a) Find \(v(2)\) and \(a(2)\).

(b) When is the object at rest?

(c) In which direction is it moving at \(t = 5\)?

(d) When does it return to its starting position?

Example — part (a)

Step 1. \(v(t) = s'(t) = 12t - 3t^{2}.\)

Step 2. \(a(t) = v'(t) = s''(t) = 12 - 6t.\)

Step 3. At \(t=2\): \(v(2) = 12(2) - 3(4) = 24 - 12 = \boxed{12 \text{ m/s}}.\)

\(a(2) = 12 - 6(2) = \boxed{0 \text{ m/s}^{2}}.\)

Interpretation: at \(t=2\) the object moves right at 12 m/s with zero acceleration — momentarily constant velocity.

Example — parts (b), (c), (d)

(b) At rest: \(v(t)=0 \Rightarrow 3t(4-t)=0 \Rightarrow t=0\) or \(t=4.\)

(c) At \(t=5\): \(v(5) = 60 - 75 = -15.\) Moving left.

(d) Return to start: \(s(t)=0 \Rightarrow t^{2}(6-t)=0 \Rightarrow t=0\) or \(t=6.\)

Returns after 6 s.

Part 2 — Absolute Extrema on an Interval

Algorithm for Absolute Maximum / Minimum

If \(f\) is differentiable on \([a, b]\), calculate \(f(x)\) at:

  • all points in \([a, b]\) where \(f'(x) = 0\),
  • the endpoints \(x = a\) and \(x = b\).

The largest of these values is the maximum; the smallest is the minimum.

The interval must be finite (closed), and \(f\) must have a derivative at every point of it.

Example — \(f(x) = -2x^{3} + 9x^{2} + 4\) on \([-1, 5]\)

Step 1. \(f'(x) = -6x^{2} + 18x = -6x(x - 3).\)

Step 2. \(f'(x) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0\) or \(x = 3.\) Both lie in \([-1, 5].\)

Step 3. Evaluate at the critical points and the endpoints.

Example — evaluate at all four candidates

\(f(-1) = -2(-1)^{3} + 9(1) + 4 = 2 + 9 + 4 = 15.\)

\(f(0) = 4.\)

\(f(3) = -2(27) + 9(9) + 4 = -54 + 81 + 4 = 31.\)

\(f(5) = -2(125) + 9(25) + 4 = -250 + 225 + 4 = -21.\)

Maximum \(f(3) = 31\) (peak)    Minimum \(f(5) = -21\) (endpoint)

Lesson: extrema can occur at a peak/valley or at an endpoint — check both.

Classwork — Extrema

Two questions from Bronston's Check Your Understanding (Lesson 3.3).

~5 min. Verbatim from the lesson page CYU block.

C3 — Absolute extrema of \(f(x) = x^{2} - 4x + 3\) on \([0, 3]\)

Step 1. \(f'(x) = 2x - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 2.\)

Step 2. Evaluate at \(x = 0, 2, 3\):

\(f(0) = 3,\;\; f(2) = -1,\;\; f(3) = 0.\)

Maximum \(= 3\) at \(x = 0\) (endpoint)    Minimum \(= -1\) at \(x = 2\) (valley)

C4 — Does the algorithm apply to \(y = \dfrac{3x}{x - 2}\) on \([-1, 3]\)?

Check: is \(y\) differentiable at every point of \([-1, 3]\)?

At \(x = 2\) the denominator is zero — \(y\) is undefined (and so has no derivative) there.

The point \(x = 2\) lies inside \([-1, 3].\)

No — the algorithm does not apply. The function must be differentiable at every point of the interval; it fails at \(x = 2.\)

Wrap-up — Lessons 3.2 + 3.3

Key ideas

  • \(v = s'\), \(a = s''\); speed \(= |v|\); speeding up when \(va > 0\), slowing down when \(va < 0\).
  • On a closed interval, absolute extrema live at points where \(f'(x) = 0\) or at the endpoints.

Need to know

  • Interval must be finite (closed); function must be differentiable throughout.
